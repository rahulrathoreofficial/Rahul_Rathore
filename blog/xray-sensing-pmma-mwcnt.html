X-Ray Sensing with PMMA-MWCNT Composites: A Deep Dive

July 27, 2026 · 10 min read

X-ray detection underpins medical imaging, security screening, industrial non-destructive testing, and astrophysical observation. Conventional detectors—gas-filled ionization chambers, scintillator-photomultiplier assemblies, and semiconductor diode arrays—offer excellent sensitivity and temporal resolution. However, these technologies often entail complex fabrication, high operating voltages, rigid form factors, and substantial costs. Polymer-based composite detectors have emerged as a compelling alternative, leveraging the mechanical flexibility, chemical stability, and processability of polymer matrices combined with the exceptional electrical and mechanical properties of carbon nanotube fillers.

Material Selection Rationale

Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), commonly known as acrylic, serves as an ideal matrix material. Its optical transparency, radiation resistance, and ease of solution processing enable uniform dispersion of nanofillers. PMMA's relatively low effective atomic number minimizes photoelectric absorption, allowing X-rays to penetrate the bulk and interact with the embedded conductive network.

Multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) constitute the functional filler. These cylindrical nanostructures, comprising concentric graphene sheets, exhibit extraordinary aspect ratios, high electrical conductivity, and remarkable mechanical strength. When dispersed within an insulating polymer matrix at concentrations approaching the percolation threshold, MWCNTs establish conductive pathways whose resistance is sensitive to mechanical deformation, thermal fluctuations, and radiation-induced chemical changes.

Fabrication Methods

The fabrication of PMMA-MWCNT composites typically proceeds via solution casting. MWCNTs are first dispersed in a solvent—commonly chloroform, toluene, or N,N-dimethylformamide—using ultrasonic agitation to overcome van der Waals aggregation. Simultaneously, PMMA beads dissolve in the same solvent. The suspensions are combined and subjected to prolonged stirring or additional sonication to achieve homogeneous distribution. The mixture is cast onto a flat substrate and allowed to dry under controlled conditions, yielding a free-standing composite film. Alternative methods include melt mixing and in-situ polymerization, each offering distinct advantages in filler dispersion and interfacial adhesion.

Structural and Electrical Characterization

Structural characterization employs scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to assess nanotube dispersion and identify agglomerates. X-ray diffraction (XRD) confirms the crystalline structure of PMMA and reveals any perturbations induced by nanotube incorporation. Raman spectroscopy probes the D-band and G-band intensities, providing insight into the structural integrity of MWCNTs following processing. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) quantifies thermal stability and approximate filler loading.

Electrical characterization measures DC conductivity as a function of filler concentration. The percolation threshold—where conductivity increases by several orders of magnitude—typically occurs between 0.1 and 5 weight percent MWCNT loading, depending on dispersion quality, aspect ratio, and alignment. Below percolation, the composite behaves as an insulator; above it, conductive pathways permit charge transport.

Sensing Mechanism

The X-ray sensing mechanism in PMMA-MWCNT composites operates through radiation-induced conductivity modulation. X-ray photons interact with the composite primarily via Compton scattering and, to a lesser extent, photoelectric absorption. These interactions generate secondary electrons that deposit energy within the polymer matrix, inducing localized heating and bond scission. The resulting thermal and chemical perturbations alter the tunneling barriers and contact resistances between adjacent nanotubes. Additionally, radiation-induced polarization effects and the creation of charge carrier traps modify the overall conductivity of the percolating network.

Upon X-ray exposure, the composite exhibits a measurable change in electrical resistance. The magnitude of this response depends on photon energy, dose rate, exposure duration, filler concentration, and composite thickness. Reproducibility and linearity of the response determine the detector's utility for quantitative dosimetry.

Results and Discussion

Research on polymer-nanotube X-ray sensors consistently demonstrates that composites near the percolation threshold exhibit the highest sensitivity. At these concentrations, the conductive network exists at a critical state where small perturbations—whether mechanical, thermal, or radiative—produce disproportionately large resistance changes. Excessive filler loading creates robust conductive pathways that resist modulation, reducing sensitivity. Conversely, insufficient loading yields an insulating composite with no measurable baseline conductivity.

The temporal response of these composites reveals a rapid initial resistance change followed by a slower recovery upon cessation of irradiation. The recovery kinetics reflect the relaxation of trapped charges and the thermal dissipation of deposited energy. Hysteresis between irradiation and recovery cycles represents a key challenge for real-time dosimetry applications.

Compared to commercial silicon detectors, PMMA-MWCNT composites offer lower sensitivity but superior flexibility, lower cost, and simpler fabrication. They are particularly suited for large-area imaging applications where conformal detectors are required—such as curved surface inspection or wearable dosimetry badges.

Conclusion and Future Scope

PMMA-MWCNT composites represent a promising materials platform for next-generation X-ray sensing. Future research should address several challenges: achieving uniform nanotube dispersion at industrially relevant scales, elucidating the long-term stability of radiation response under cyclic exposure, and developing theoretical models that quantitatively link microstructural parameters to macroscopic sensing behavior. The integration of these composites with flexible electronics and wireless readout circuits could enable distributed sensor networks for radiation monitoring in medical, industrial, and security contexts.
